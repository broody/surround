# Fair play

A proof shows that every move in a game was legal and signed by the player
whose turn it was. It can't show who *chose* the move. A player can sign
moves that KataGo picked for them, and the chain can't tell the difference.

So Surround analyzes games in its backend. When an account's games show, over
time, that it's playing an AI's moves, the account is **labeled**. It isn't
banned and can keep playing, but everyone who meets it sees the label:

> **Possible unregistered bot.** This account's games match AI play more closely
> than human play at its rank.

The approach borrows from the chess servers that have fought this problem
longest. As on Lichess and Chess.com, the evidence is statistical and is
gathered across many games, and a person reviews it before any label goes on.

## Registered and unregistered bots

Bots are welcome on Surround as long as they're open about it. The
[AI anchors](/ai-anchors) are registered bots: they're named as AI everywhere
they appear, and their ratings are pinned. An **unregistered** bot is a human
account whose moves come from an AI. The label exists so that you know which
of the two you're playing.

## What we analyze

Every game played through Surround's keepers is analyzed after it ends. The
analysis uses only what the game itself contains: its moves and the time
spent on each, which the referee stamped and attested.

Each position is analyzed by KataGo at high strength, and each move is also
scored by KataGo's **human SL model** for the player's rank, the same model
the [AI anchors](/ai-anchors#how-they-play) play with. Popular Go AIs (KataGo,
Leela Zero and their descendants) agree with one another on most moves that
matter, so measuring agreement with one of them catches assistance from any.

### Signals

- **Point loss for the rank.** Every move gives up some points against the
  best move. We measure a player's average point loss, a measure Go
  researchers prefer to win rate because a close game swings the win rate on
  tiny mistakes. We then compare it with how much humans at that rank
  actually lose. A 15k who loses half a point per move over many games is
  playing far above their rank. This is Go's version of the intrinsic
  performance rating that Chess.com measures.
- **Agreement where it's hard.** Matching the AI's top move proves nothing by
  itself: some moves are forced, and many are obvious to any player. We count
  agreement only in positions where the human model is unsure, where
  players at the rank genuinely spread across several moves, and where the
  AI's choice is one humans rarely find. Agreement in the opening and in
  routine endgame moves counts for little.
- **Human or engine?** For each move we compare two likelihoods: that a human
  at the player's rank would play it, and that a strong AI would. Human play,
  even strong human play, leaves a recognizable trail of human-shaped choices.
  A long run of moves the human model finds unlikely and the AI finds natural
  is the clearest signal we have. It's the idea behind Lichess's Kaladin,
  which looks for play uncharacteristic of the player.
- **Timing.** People move quickly when a move is obvious and think when it's
  hard. Someone relaying moves from an AI tends to take the same time on
  every move, or thinks longest when the position is simple. Ranked games are
  timed by the referee, so every move's time is known to the millisecond.
- **The player's own history.** A sudden jump in move quality, unlike the
  account's earlier games, counts more than steady strong play. So does a
  run of wins against much stronger opponents.

No single signal labels an account. Each game gets a score from all of them,
and the scores add up across the account's recent games.

### What isn't counted

- **Short games.** Games too short to rate are too short to judge.
- **Games after a resumption,** when both players are capturing dead stones
  and the best move is plain.
- **Forced moves**, such as answering atari or retaking an obvious capture.

## From signal to label

1. **Analysis.** Each finished game is queued for analysis. KataGo runs in the
   backend, never in the players' browsers.
2. **Accumulation.** The account's game scores accumulate over a rolling
   window. One game, however strong, never flags an account: a good day and
   a good game happen to everyone.
3. **Review.** An account that crosses the threshold is flagged for review. A
   person looks at the games and the evidence before a label goes on, because
   the software only flags. (Lichess works the same way: its detection tools
   flag accounts, and only its moderators mark them.)
4. **Label.** The account is labeled as a possible unregistered bot.

We don't publish the thresholds or every feature we use. Chess.com and
Lichess keep theirs confidential too: a published threshold is a target to
play just under.

## What the label does

- **It's visible.** The label shows on the player's profile, in the lobby, on
  the pairing offer before you sign a game's terms, and beside their name
  during the game. You can decline a game against a labeled account.
- **It doesn't stop play.** A labeled account can still queue, open tables and
  play rated games. We would rather show players the evidence than decide
  for them.
- **It isn't onchain.** The label is backend data, not a contract flag. The
  chain records results and ratings, not suspicions, so a labeled account's
  [rating](/ranking/rated-games) changes by the same rules as anyone else's.

## Mistakes and appeals

Statistical detection gives false positives, and the cost of a wrong label
falls on a player who did nothing wrong. That's why accounts need many games
of evidence, why a person reviews every flag, and why labels can be
appealed. On appeal the account's games are reviewed again, and a label that
doesn't hold up is removed.

Strong players who are new to Surround are the likeliest false positives:
they play far above their [starting band](/ranking/ranks#starting-bands) at
first. Their history quickly catches up with them, and the point-loss signal
is measured against their rating as it rises, not against where they
started.

## Where detection stops

- **Selective help is harder to see.** A player who consults an AI for only a
  few critical moves leaves a weaker trail than one who copies every move.
  Those moves still tend to fall in hard positions, where we look most
  closely, and the evidence builds up across games.
- **Detection is never proof.** The label says *possible* for a reason.
- **Proofs and signatures don't help here.** They make a game's moves honest,
  not its players. See [Settling a game](/settlement/settling#trust-assumptions).
